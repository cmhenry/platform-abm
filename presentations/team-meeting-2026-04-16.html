Platform Architecture as Political System

Tiebout sorting, extremist cycling, and structural protection
in multi-platform ecosystems

Team meeting · April 16, 2026

10 minutes + discussion. Goal: validate the framing, theory, and ABM strategy before submission. Two pillars: (1) how platform-system structure shapes welfare outcomes; (2) how that structure produces the empirically observed extremist movement patterns.

The empirical puzzle

Extremist communities don't disappear after bans — they cycle. The observable pattern has two signatures:

WHERE they end up

Extremists concentrate stably on specific platform types, overrepresented 3–10× relative to platform capacity. This is not random drift.

HOW they move

Movement is bursty: 94–100% of outflow occurs in mass departure events. This looks coordinated but recurs even when platforms are unconnected.
Both signatures are system-level properties. Neither follows from individual-platform accounts (content moderation, network lock-in, coordination failure).
Frame the puzzle as having two parts from the start — the WHERE and the HOW. Standard deplatforming literature addresses neither: it treats the destination as unpredictable and treats the movement as coordinated. Both of those assumptions are wrong, and the Tiebout model explains why.

Theoretical frame: Tiebout sorting

Tiebout (1956): citizens choose among competing jurisdictions based on the public goods bundle each offers. The equilibrium is system-level — it depends on the composition of alternatives, not any single jurisdiction's policy.

Platforms are quasi-governments offering governance bundles. Communities sort across them by voting with their feet. Concentration and cycling are sorting equilibria — not coordination failures.

Key move: the unit of analysis shifts from individual platforms to the platform system — governance type mix, jurisdictional diversity, and the structural positions available to different community types.

Two features make the Tiebout analogy tractable: (1) communities respond to a governance bundle, not a single policy; (2) the sorting equilibrium depends on what else is in the ecosystem. The question for the room: does the bundle-response assumption hold empirically for extremist communities, or only for fandom communities (which is where most migration evidence comes from)?

The model

D Direct voting

Majority rule; all neighbors interact.
Reddit, early forums.

K Coalition formation

Preference blocs compete for platform control.
Discord, federated platforms.

A Algorithmic

SVD-based recommendation by affinity.
TikTok, YouTube, Facebook.

Community types: mainstream; ideologues (α=2, preference-seeking); griefers (α=10, target-seeking). Extremist utility: ubase + α·(nmainstream/total). Mainstream utility: ubase − (αi·nideo + αg·ngrief)/total.

3×3×3 factorial: Np ∈ {3, 9, 27} × ρe ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.15} × α ∈ {2, 5, 10}. 900 communities · 200 iterations · tmax=100.

Don't linger here. The governance typology is motivated by real platform families. The utility formula is the core mechanism: griefers are explicitly target-seeking (high α means the mainstream term dominates their payoff), ideologues are preference-seeking (low α means base utility dominates). The factorial design tests whether system composition or threat intensity is the dominant driver.

System structure II — The coalition governance paradox

Without extremists

Coalition governance is mediocre: slower convergence, lower average welfare than direct or algorithmic platforms.

Under extremist threat

Coalition governance is the strongest firewall: mainstream welfare declines 0.22 units as α doubles; direct platforms lose 3.60.
Mechanism: extremists share coherent preference vectors → win plurality voting → set extremist platform policy → mainstream communities depart → parasitism channel severed. Enclave homogeneity: 0.92–0.98. No coordinated moderation required.

Enclave settling: median 23 steps at Np=27, α=10. Post-settlement disruptions re-sort within a few steps.

The coalition paradox is the clearest governance-type finding. The mechanism is purely structural: extremists' preference coherence gives them bloc-voting power, which sets extremist policy, which pushes mainstream communities out. Once the mainstream leaves, the parasitism channel is gone. This happens automatically — it's not a moderation decision. Question for room: does this mechanism have analogs in how Discord servers or federated platforms (like Mastodon instances) actually behave?

Extremist movement I — Stable concentration on direct platforms

~50% of all extremists on direct platforms at simulation end
3–10× overrepresentation relative to platform capacity
stable across all α and Np values
Extremists are not assigned to direct platforms — they migrate there. Algorithmic platforms partially insulate communities by affinity grouping. Coalition platforms segregate extremists into enclaves. Only direct voting leaves all communities unprotected under a shared policy.

The 50% concentration figure is invariant to extremist prevalence (ρe=0.05, 0.10, 0.15) and parasitism intensity. Governance structure, not threat level, sets the attractor.

This is the WHERE finding. The stability of 50% concentration across all factor levels is the key. It's not that more extremists → more concentration on direct platforms. The rate is fixed by the governance structure. Direct platforms are the optimal jurisdiction for parasitic extraction regardless of how many extremists are in the system.

Extremist movement II — Raiding cycles are emergent

94–100% of extremist outflow occurs in burst events (≥10 departures/step). Median burst: 31 communities. Median inter-burst interval: 9 steps. No community coordinates this.

Tipping dynamic: Extremists accumulate on a direct platform → shift policy toward their preferences → mainstream utility drops → mainstream departs → parasitism payoff collapses → extremists discover better options simultaneously → mass departure → repeat.

Behavioral type shapes the pattern:

Ideologue (low α)
Large concentrated bursts (median 31). No escalation trend (p=0.34). Policy-driven mass departures.
Griefer (high fg)
Smaller bursts (median 15 at fg=0.75), strong escalation (slope=1.27, p<0.001). Continuous disruption.
The HOW finding. What looks like organized raids is a decentralized tipping dynamic. The policy implication: targeting coordination channels doesn't address the structural incentive. The mixed-type (f_g) analysis is new this week — it shows the ideologue-to-griefer transition is continuous, not categorical, which validates using α as a behavioral proxy.

Discussion

1. Is Tiebout the right frame?
The core assumption: communities respond to governance bundles and sort accordingly. Does that hold for extremist communities, or only for the fandom-migration cases cited in the literature?
2. Is the governance typology right?
Direct / coalition / algorithmic maps to Reddit / Discord / TikTok-era platforms. Are there cases this misclassifies, or a fourth type that matters?
3. What empirical tests are tractable?
The model predicts: ~50% extremist concentration on direct-type platforms; burst-structured outflow; governance-type welfare differentials. Which of these can be checked against existing data?

Target venues: Computational Communication Research, J. Computational Social Science. Scope claim: sufficient conditions for the cycling pattern to arise from decentralized sorting. Sensitivity analysis currently running on HPC.

On question 1: the Chandrasekharan et al. and Fiesler et al. evidence is from fandom communities facing technical barriers — not strategically sophisticated extremist groups. One reviewer flagged this; worth an honest answer from the room. On question 3: burst timing and concentration ratios could potentially be tested with platform API data.
