The puzzle: deplatforming doesn't work

Extremist communities don't disappear after bans — they cycle. Concentration on one platform → raid elsewhere → retreat → repeat.

Standard explanations invoke coordination or network lock-in. Both are individual-platform accounts. Neither explains why the cycling is system-level and self-reproducing.

What's missing: the role of platform-system architecture — the mix of governance types across which communities can sort.

You all know the empirical puzzle: Infowars, the chans, Gab, Parler, Truth Social. The pattern recurs across cases. The current literature explains this at the level of individual communities or individual platforms. That's not wrong, but it's incomplete — the cycling is a property of the system, not of any single community or platform. That's the gap I'm trying to fill.

Theoretical move: platforms as jurisdictions

Tiebout (1956): in a system of competing local governments, citizens vote with their feet — choosing jurisdictions by the public goods bundle they offer.

Platforms are quasi-governments offering governance bundles. Communities are mobile agents that sort across them. The cycling pattern is a structural equilibrium — not a coordination failure.

This reframes the unit of analysis from individual platforms to the platform system — what matters is the mix of governance types, not any one platform's policy.

The Tiebout move is doing a lot of work here. Two features make it apt: (1) communities respond to a governance *bundle*, not a single policy; (2) the sorting outcome depends on what else is in the ecosystem. Whether this framing is convincing to reviewers is one of the things I want your read on. The alternative would be a network-theoretic frame — I'll explain why I rejected that if you ask.

The model (briefly)

D Direct voting

Communities set policy by majority rule. All neighbors interact. Real analogue: Reddit, early forums.

K Coalition formation

Preference blocs compete for platform control via hill-climbing search. Real analogue: Discord servers, federated platforms.

A Algorithmic

SVD-based recommendation groups communities by affinity. Real analogue: TikTok, YouTube, Facebook.

Two extremist subtypes: ideologues (α=2, preference-driven) and griefers (α=10, target-seeking). 3×3×3 factorial: Np × ρe × α · 200 iterations · 900 communities.

Don't get lost in the model spec. The three governance types map to real platform families — that's what motivates the typology. The "α" parameter is the behavioral lever: low α = ideologues who care about policy alignment, high α = griefers who care about accessing mainstream targets. The factorial design lets us test how these three factors interact — is the system compositional, or is it driven by one dominant factor?

The bootstrap CI for the premium at α=10 is 0.98 [0.93, 1.03] — economically meaningful.

Finding 2 — The coalition paradox

Without extremists

Coalition governance is mediocre — slower to converge, lower average welfare than direct or algorithmic platforms.

Under extremist threat

Coalition governance becomes the strongest firewall. Mainstream welfare on coalition platforms declines only 0.22 units as α doubles; direct platforms lose 3.60.

Mechanism: extremists' coherent preference vectors win plurality voting → homogeneous extremist enclaves form → mainstream communities depart → parasitism channel is structurally severed. Enclave homogeneity: 0.92–0.98.

Communities don't anticipate extremist behavior. No coordinated moderation required. The coalition structure produces enclave segregation endogenously.

This is the finding I'm most excited about, and the one I want your read on. It's counterintuitive: the governance type that looks worst at baseline is actually the best firewall under threat. The mechanism is clear in the model — extremists are a coherent bloc that wins plurality voting, which sets extremist policy, which drives mainstream communities out. Once they're out, the parasitism channel is gone. The question for you: is this a plausible description of how coalition-based platforms (like Discord servers) actually behave? That's the empirical validity question.

Finding 3 — The raiding cycle is emergent, not coordinated

94–100% of extremist outflow occurs in mass departure bursts. Median burst: 31 communities. Median inter-burst interval: 9 steps.
No community plans this. It's a tipping dynamic.

The mechanism: Extremists accumulate on a direct platform → shift policy toward their preferences → mainstream communities' utility drops → mainstream departs → parasitism payoff collapses → extremists simultaneously discover better options → mass departure → hit algorithmic platforms → algorithm re-sorts → disperse → repeat.

Behavioral type matters: Ideologue populations produce large, concentrated, non-escalating bursts. Griefer populations produce smaller but continuously escalating bursts (slope=1.27, p<0.001 at fg=0.75).

This is the policy-critical finding. What looks like organized raids — coordinated mass movements — is an emergent property of decentralized sorting, not strategic planning. The implication: if you try to disrupt the coordination (e.g., by monitoring Signal channels where extremists plan raids), you're solving the wrong problem. The structural incentives produce the same behavior regardless of whether anyone coordinates. The mixed ideologue/griefer finding (f_g analysis) is new results added this week — shows the transition is continuous, not categorical.

So what — and what the model can't say

Platform-system design, not individual moderation decisions, determines whether mainstream communities are structurally protected.

The policy levers: Governance type composition of the ecosystem; platform diversification; structural separation of governance regimes (not deplatforming, content moderation, or coordination disruption).

Honest limits: The model is not calibrated to real platforms. Results establish sufficient conditions for the cycling pattern to arise — not predictive claims. Sensitivity analysis underway (HPC).

I want to be explicit about what the model can and can't claim. The core theoretical contribution is: "structural incentives alone are sufficient to produce this pattern." That's a weaker claim than "this explains what we observe on real platforms," but it's defensible and falsifiable. The sensitivity analysis will test whether the key findings hold across parameter variations — that's the piece reviewers will push on hardest.
